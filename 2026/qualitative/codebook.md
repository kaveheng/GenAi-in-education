# Codebook: open-ended reflections

**Source item:** POST-O – *What was the most useful part of being questioned by the AI, and what was one limitation?*

**Approach:** Inductive thematic coding supported by keyword checks, carried out by one coder and reviewed with a second researcher. Themes are non-mutually exclusive: one response could receive several useful-aspect and limitation themes, so percentages do not sum to 100%. Counts by AI role are descriptive only.

## Useful-aspect themes

| Theme | Definition | AI Student (n = 49) | AI Tutor (n = 47) | Total (N = 96) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Follow-up probing / elaboration | Students valued being pushed to explain, justify, narrow, or develop answers further. | 12 (24%) | 18 (38%) | 30 (31%) |
| Critical reflection / challenge | AI questioning made students test claims, notice possible errors, and think more critically. | 22 (45%) | 7 (15%) | 29 (30%) |
| Deeper understanding / learning | Students connected the interaction to improved understanding of papers, concepts, and comparisons. | 13 (27%) | 10 (21%) | 23 (24%) |
| Engagement / interactive format | The activity was described as interactive, fun, active, or more engaging than a conventional seminar. | 13 (27%) | 8 (17%) | 21 (22%) |
| Feedback / guidance / quick response | Students valued direct feedback, quick responses, correction, or guidance. | 10 (20%) | 6 (13%) | 16 (17%) |
| Group discussion / collaboration | Students mentioned that the activity stimulated discussion or joint work in the group. | 8 (16%) | 4 (9%) | 12 (12%) |
| Other useful aspect | Useful aspect was positive but did not fit the main themes. | 2 (4%) | 7 (15%) | 9 (9%) |
| Identify gaps / blind spots | The AI helped students notice what they had not understood or would otherwise miss. | 1 (2%) | 4 (9%) | 5 (5%) |

## Limitation themes

| Theme | Definition | AI Student (n = 49) | AI Tutor (n = 47) | Total (N = 96) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No clear limitation mentioned | Student explicitly reported no limitation or did not identify one. | 10 (20%) | 13 (28%) | 23 (24%) |
| AI misunderstanding / accuracy / trust | Students reported that AI sometimes misunderstood, gave incorrect interpretations, or needed verification. | 16 (33%) | 6 (13%) | 22 (23%) |
| Communication / wording / input barrier | Students reported unclear wording, semantic ambiguity, typing/language barriers, or difficulty formulating answers. | 11 (22%) | 6 (13%) | 17 (18%) |
| Time pressure / too short | Students wanted more time to discuss, respond, or continue the interaction. | 7 (14%) | 8 (17%) | 15 (16%) |
| Repetition / stuck / too picky | Students experienced repeated questions, fixation on details, or overly strict/picky evaluation. | 4 (8%) | 10 (21%) | 14 (15%) |
| Other limitation | Limitation was mentioned but did not fit the main themes. | 4 (8%) | 6 (13%) | 10 (10%) |
| Limited scope / rigidity / depth | Some comments pointed to rigid interaction, insufficient scope, vague demands, or limited novelty/depth. | 7 (14%) | 1 (2%) | 8 (8%) |
| Stress / frustration / harshness | Some experienced the interaction as stressful, frustrating, or harsh. | 3 (6%) | 1 (2%) | 4 (4%) |
| Reduced human / social interaction | A few students missed human interaction or preferred discussion with a person. | 1 (2%) | 2 (4%) | 3 (3%) |

## Coding notes

- **Unit of analysis:** One student free-text response.
- **Coding type:** Thematic coding of comments into useful-aspect themes and limitation themes.
- **Non-mutually exclusive coding:** A single response could receive several themes. Percentages therefore show the share of responses mentioning each theme, not a distribution that totals 100%.
- **Interpretation caution:** Themes by AI role are descriptive counts only. For claims about role differences, use these themes together with the quantitative AI-role comparison and sample size information.
- **Recommended reporting:** Report the dominant themes with counts/percentages and include short illustrative comments. Then connect the themes to quantitative results such as satisfaction, engagement, and perceived challenge.

Counts exclude one participant who declined consent for research use (N = 96).

Individual responses and quotations are not published, in line with the consent given by participants (aggregated or anonymised use only).
